The read

A 39.5-total division game where both offenses have been stripped of their best player. Lamar Jackson is out (ruled out Friday, per Rotowire — he hasn't practiced since the Week 4 ankle), so Tyler Huntley starts a Ravens offense that already passed on only 47.2% of its snaps. Cleveland ruled Quinshon Judkins out after a pre-game workout Sunday morning (RotoBaller/FantasyPros, Oct 18), leaving Jaleel McLaughlin and Audric Estime behind a run game that has posted negative rush EPA in all five games. That makes this a Henry-and-clock script with Cleveland throwing by necessity.

Why

Cleveland ranks 30th in rush EPA allowed and 23rd in yards per carry allowed (4.48), and the interior that is supposed to fix it is gone: Mike Hall out, Mason Graham not expected to play (Cabot/Schefter, Sunday), Maliek Collins limited, Carson Schwesinger a pre-game call. Baltimore is averaging 5.07 yards a carry. That is the matchup the whole game runs through, and the model historically under-rates backs against bad run defenses.

The other half: Cleveland's pass defense ranks 5th in pass EPA allowed but 20th in passing yards allowed (239.8) — it takes the ball away and gets off the field, it does not erase yardage. So Baltimore's receiving lines, set low for a backup quarterback, are still live on volume while Huntley's team total is capped by attempts.

Cleveland's own path is Watson's scrambles (38, 22, 45, 22, 40 rushing) and Denzel Boston, who has played 89-95% of snaps and taken over half the air yards every week, against a Baltimore secondary that has allowed 3+ explosive passes in four of five games.

What would change it

Baltimore falling behind early — their Week 4 first half was 69.7% pass when the game was tied. A Schwesinger activation firming the run fits. Any late word that Estime is the lead back rather than a committee. And Kalshi's player prices are thin; check the volume behind a number before taking it.

The short version

Baltimore scored 10 points in the first 10 minutes and nothing for the next 50. Cleveland, an offense averaging -0.079 field EPA on the season, didn't have to be good — it had to stop giving the ball away, and for the first time in three weeks it did. The Browns won 13-10 on a 37-yard kick at 0:00 that existed only because Baltimore blocked the 42-yarder and then got flagged for being offside. The model had the right game (projected total 39.4, actual 23) and the wrong side, because it gave the Ravens 21 points and 130 rushing yards off a Lamar Jackson efficiency profile with Lamar Jackson ruled out.

How it unfolded

The first Ravens drive was the whole case for the favorite: 11 plays, 66 yards, 6:49, a fourth-and-2 touchdown throw to Josh Cuevas at Q1 8:14. The next one was free money — a 29-yard completion to Cuevas with a 15-yard roughing-the-passer tacked on — and it produced a 34-yard field goal at 4:40. Ten-nothing, and that was the last time Baltimore's offense did anything. Over the next seven possessions it punted six times and missed a 49-yard field goal, with three-and-outs bracketing both of Cleveland's fourth-quarter scores.

Cleveland spent the first half getting sacked (three of its five) and the third quarter marching: an 11-play, 57-yard drive featuring a converted fourth-and-1 at Q3 12:41, which should have reached first-and-goal before Barber's holding penalty erased a 14-yard McLaughlin run at Q3 9:00. A 48-yarder instead of seven. Then the game turned on a single carry — Henry, first-and-5, stripped by Schwesinger and recovered by Tyson Campbell at the Baltimore 35 (Q4 12:45, -5.04 EPA). Eight plays later Estime punched it in from the 2 and it was 10-10. Cleveland missed a 48-yarder at 3:44, Baltimore punted from midfield after a delay of game, and Watson's 33-yard strike to Jeudy at 0:42 did the rest.

Why the game diverged from the projection

Two numbers. First, the blended 4.61 yards per carry: Baltimore got 3.8, Henry got 3.4 with four tackles for loss, and the team's rush EPA was -0.564 against an average faced box of 6.47 — a light box that still won. The engine's 5.07 YPC input belongs to an offense with a quarterback who has to be accounted for on every snap. Second, 8.99 yards per attempt blended down to 7.47: Huntley got 5.3, on the projected number of throws, because he was hit nine times, sacked three times (23.7% sack-or-hit against a Cleveland blitz rate of only 28.9%) and went 1-for-6 on deep balls. Volume was projected beautifully; efficiency was a Jackson number applied to a Huntley game.

Cleveland's miss was the mirror. The engine expected 31.5 attempts; Watson threw 26 and the Browns ran 25 times despite trailing for 47 of 63 plays, because rushing was their efficient facet (48% rush success, -0.074 EPA/rush) and dropping back wasn't (-0.205 EPA/dropback).

The plan against the game

The plan's underdog paragraph is nearly a transcript of the fourth quarter — explosive pass, scrambles, Baltimore penalties, a late kick — but it got there on a different set of legs. Its highest-confidence call, Henry over 89.5 on a depleted Cleveland interior, was the one thing that never materialized, and both of its leans inverted: Baltimore threw more than its season rate, Cleveland threw less. The two calls built on role rather than matchup (Fannin under, Huntley pass-yards under) came in. Boston's call is the instructive one: the role held exactly as written (92.1% of snaps, 41.2% of air yards) and produced one catch for 15, which is what happens when the quarterback is 1-of-4 deep under a 37.8% pressure rate.

Player by player

Flowers was the only Ravens pass-catcher the projection recognized: 11 targets, 6-66, 38.8% of the air yards, including the 31-yard conversion on third-and-11 at Q3 1:45. Behind him the depth chart has moved — Bateman played 29.8% of snaps and saw one target, while Ja'Kobi Lane (54.4%, 4-40) and Cuevas (40.4%, 4-44 and the touchdown, with Hibner out) took the work. Henry's 17-58 and the fumble are the headline; note also that Justice Hill took 42.1% of snaps and three targets, so the checkdown share the model gave Henry isn't his.

For Cleveland: McLaughlin was the actual lead back (9 carries, 4-for-4 receiving, 61.9% snaps) while Estime took the short-yardage/goal-line split and the touchdown on 2.1 yards a carry. Jeudy was 4-for-4 for 69 at a 61.9% snap share; Concepcion played 81% of snaps and led the team in targets, which is the opposite of the 'collapsed role' the pregame read assumed.

The defenses

No pressure or coverage charting landed for this game, so the proxies: Baltimore generated three sacks and nine hits on 37 dropbacks while blitzing on 28.9% of snaps — a real four-man rush, with Campbell, Smith, Martin, Robinson/Madubuike and Hendrickson all getting home. Cleveland did it from a lighter front, averaging 4.34 rushers and an 18.4% blitz rate, and still hit Huntley nine times with two sacks in a two-minute stretch of the second quarter. Both red-zone units did what the engine said they would: three combined trips in 120 snaps, two touchdowns, five field-goal attempts.

What the model should take from this

The quarterback-dependence of Baltimore's rushing inputs is the actionable lesson. The engine blended a 5.07 YPC that came with Jackson's threat attached; without it the Ravens got 3.8 and abandoned the run, throwing on 72.5% of their snaps while leading. That's a correction to make inside the inputs, not after the fact. The Bateman snap share is a second: it's a role change with corroboration (Lane and Walker both out-snapped him), not a one-week quirk.

Two things should not move. The Henry fumble is a single strip from an offense with one turnover in five previous games, and Watson missing a 29.5-yard rushing line by half a yard with six scrambles for 26 is noise in the right direction. The penalty read, however, is worth more weight than it had: both teams were tagged for pre-snap fouls repeatedly coming in, both committed four or five here, and the game's last snap — a blocked game-winner returned to the kicker by a Baltimore offside — is the clearest illustration all season of why that one category persists.
