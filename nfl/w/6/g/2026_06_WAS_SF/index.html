The short version

San Francisco scored on its first possession, led for every second after 10:58 of the first quarter, built it to 20-3 by halftime and then produced nothing after the 6:01 mark of the third. Washington out-threw its projection by 89 yards, scored twice in the second half and died twice inside the SF 20 — turnover on downs at the 13 in the second quarter, turnover on downs at the 17 with 2:00 left. Final 23-17: SF wins, SF doesn't cover 7.5, 40 points stays well under 51.5. The model's three market calls all hit; the player and team layers took their damage entirely on the Washington side of the ledger.

How it unfolded

SF's first two drives were 64 and 77 yards and were essentially two plays: Purdy to Mike Evans for 24 on 3rd-and-4, then Purdy to Evans for 54 to the WAS 18. Evans, a DNP all week with ribs, played 40% of the snaps and finished with 94 yards on five targets and a 39.4% air-yards share. The second drive survived because Purdy scrambled seven yards on 4th-and-3 at the WAS 11 (+3.10 EPA) — SF went 2-for-2 on fourth down, Washington 1-for-4. Washington's only answer in the half was a 27-yard McLaurin catch into a 51-yard field goal, and McLaurin was then lost on a Q1 2:16 sack.

Washington got its break — Luvu recovered a McCaffrey catch-fumble at the WAS 19, a -4.36 EPA play that took seven points off SF's board — and went four plays, eight yards, punt. SF answered with a 16-play, 89-yard, 6:13 drive that absorbed two offensive holdings and a 4th-and-1 sneak to make it 20-3 with eighteen seconds left in the half.

The second half was all Washington in possession and all stalemate in result. A 12-play, 56-yard drive with a converted 4th-and-5 to Diggs made it 23-10; an 11-play, 66-yard drive capped by a 9-yard throw to White made it 23-17 with 9:22 left. Then the two plays that settled it: McNichols' 31-yard touchdown run erased by a Chris Paul face mask at 4:19, and Daniels' incompletion to Burks on 4th-and-3 from the SF 17 at 2:00 — a 0.221 win-probability swing, the largest of the night.

Why the game diverged from the projection

The engine blended Washington to 62.3 plays at a 55.8% pass rate — 32.5 attempts, 27.5 carries, 114.9 rush yards. Washington ran 64 plays at 70.3% passing with 17 carries for 53 yards. Every one of those 64 snaps came while trailing; the pass_rate_by_state table has zero plays tied or leading. Part of that is pure script, but part is real: the 15 designed runs produced -0.175 EPA/rush with no 10+ gain, 0.8 yards a carry up the middle, and the one explosive run came back on a flag.

The pass-yard overshoot is the same mechanism twice over — volume plus efficiency. Daniels went 29-of-40 with +17.6 CPOE and 5-of-9 on deep balls against a secondary the inputs had rated as the hardest in the matchup to complete against. What did not happen was the quarterback run game: SF's front, without Bosa, hit him on 28.9% of dropbacks and took four sacks for -39 yards. The 66.7 projected rush yards became 10. San Francisco's own projection was essentially clean — 29 attempts, 29 carries, 62 plays, 390 yards — and missed only on points, because the fumble and three dead second-half drives cost it possessions, not efficiency.

The plan against the game

The plan's shape was right and one of its mechanisms was exactly backwards. SF leading early on passing and red-zone finishing, Washington walled off on the ground, the Commanders' pass rate climbing past 65%, average pace — all of that landed (checks: 121 plays vs 121.5 league, both leans held). But the plan's single highest-confidence lever, Daniels' legs against a thin edge rotation, inverted: the pressure arrived, the scrambles didn't, and the yardage the plan expected on the ground arrived through the air instead, breaking both U1 and D6 at once. The penalty read inverted too — one Washington pre-snap flag against eleven San Francisco penalties for 88 yards — but that one looks like the bounce, not the read.

Player by player

Okonkwo caught all seven of his targets for 69 yards on a 76% snap share; 34 of those yards were YAC, which is what a pressure valve looks like. Burks inherited McLaurin's field-stretching work (75 air yards, 28.1% share) and produced 66 yards and the fourth-down incompletion that ended it. Antonio Williams was the cleanest projection in the game — 3 catches on 4 targets against 2.9 and 4.2. White's projected carries became targets: 7 for 27 on the ground, 4 catches for 30 and the touchdown.

For SF, McCaffrey was the usage story — 16 carries for 64 with both goal-line scores on left guard, plus ten targets and nine catches for 53. Kittle drew a team-high eight targets on 88.7% of snaps. Evans' line is the lesson in low-volume, high-aDOT roles: five targets on a snap count, 94 yards.

The defenses

Charting for pressures and coverage hasn't landed, so read the proxies. San Francisco's front, supposedly rushless, generated four sacks and nine hits with Odighizuwa, Mykel Williams, Halton, Height and White sharing it — Washington's 89% shotgun rate and 7.8 aDOT say a quarterback getting the ball out and still getting hit. Washington's front held: SF managed 4.3 yards a carry with no explosive runs and put only three hits on Purdy. Both secondaries gave up four 20+ passes. The difference was at the goal line — SF 3-for-4 with +0.253 EPA inside the 20, Washington 2-for-4 with -0.408 while posting +0.083 between the 20s.

The blueprint

Washington's is now four weeks deep and loud: explosive passes over the top (four allowed in each of weeks 2, 3, 5 and here) and touchdowns conceded on trips inside the 20 (the red zone defense tag in four of five prior games). San Francisco had exactly the profile to cash it — 8.42 Y/A, +0.143 field EPA, 0.833 red-zone TD rate — and did, on 62 plays. On the other side, Washington's own failure is finishing, not movement: positive field EPA, negative red-zone EPA, 3-of-13 on third down.

San Francisco gets beaten the way Arizona and Seattle nearly beat it: take the yards between the 20s against a bend defense, collect its penalties, and survive until the lead-protecting offense goes dormant. Washington had the passing efficiency to try it and did, but it needed one more red-zone touchdown and one more third down, and it has neither the finishing rate nor the run game to buy them.

What the model should take from this

When a sub-league-average rushing offense is a 7+ point dog with its lead back out, the blended carry figure is fiction — cut toward 17-20 and move the attempts up. Mobile-QB rush props should be priced off realized pressure, not off the missing edge rusher: the scramble yards and the sack yards are the same budget. And 'no pass rush' is the least persistent tag in this dossier — two weeks of it bought SF a reputation it demolished in one game.
