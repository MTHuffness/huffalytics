The plan said

New England should lead early and play the possession game it played against Buffalo and Las Vegas: long drives, short throws, a three-way backfield, and fourth-down aggression inside scoring range. The Jets' answer is Braelon Allen and a handful of concentrated shots to Garrett Wilson, with their drive count the limiting factor — penalties and third downs have ended New York's possessions all season. What happened

New England won 26-19 as a 7.5-point favorite — the Jets covered and the game went over 43.5 by a point and a half — and the entire gap was finishing: the Jets outgained their projection and scored zero touchdowns on three red-zone trips plus two failed fourth downs in New England territory.

The plan read the shape of this game well: New England leading throughout, winning short with a three-man backfield and a run lean, points arriving as field goals as often as touchdowns, the Jets' offense limited rather than explosive. Where it went wrong is the mechanism behind the Jets' limitation — it bet on pre-snap penalties and third downs ending New York's drives, and instead New York cleaned up its flags (5 for 35, 2 pre-snap) and died at the 20 and on fourth down. That inversion flipped three calls at once: Smith's yardage, his interception, and the Under. The call record (4-6) understates the read; most of the misses were efficiency and target-tree surprises (Cooper, Brown's return, Henry's 39 YAC), not a misread of the script.

The script: New England followed the favorite path almost literally from the second quarter on: it leaned on Stevenson and Kiner between the 20s (36 carries, checks confirm a run lean at a 47.1% pass rate against a 52.5% season rate), Maye finished with 32 attempts and 235 modest yards, and the Jets threw from behind — 45 of their 61 snaps came trailing and their second-half pass rate was 73.1%. But the plan's two stated limiters both failed: the Patriots did not score on their first two or three possessions (punt, punt, then the 42-yard Doubs shot off a short field), and the Jets' drive count was never the constraint — 61 plays, 29:49 of possession, and 345 yards. Pace was called average and came in fast (128 plays vs 121.5 league), the NYJ lean was called pass and graded balanced (55.0% against a 55.3% season rate), and the total the plan expected to land 'well under' went over by a point and a half on six field goals.

The short version

New England 26, New York 19 — a seven-point game under a 7.5-point line, so the Jets covered, and 45 total points cleared a 43.5 total that the projection had nailed to the decimal. The team totals could hardly have been better: 18 projected against 19 scored, 25.5 against 26. What the projection could not have known is how those points would be assembled. New York gained 349 yards, reached New England territory on seven of its ten real possessions, and scored no touchdowns inside the 20 — four Sanders field goals, two failed fourth downs in plus territory, and an offensive pass interference that erased a 14-yard touchdown in the first quarter. Its only touchdown came from 48 yards out. That is the whole margin.

How it unfolded

The Jets opened with a 13-play, 6:43 field-goal drive and then handed the game away in the next ten minutes: a two-sack series that lost 16 yards, a 53-yard punt wiped out by a player-out-of-bounds penalty giving New England the ball at the NYJ 42, and two plays later a 42-yard Maye-to-Doubs touchdown (+4.12 EPA) — on which Doubs was injured and essentially disappeared (2.9% snap share).

The second quarter was where New York actually lost it. Two drives of 51 and 58 yards ended on fourth down inside New England's half: Allen for one yard on 4th-and-2 at the NE 30 (-3.17 EPA), then Smith's sneak stuffed at the NE 39 on 4th-and-1 (-3.19). New England answered the second with an 11-play field-goal drive that converted its own 4th-and-1, then went 61 yards in 1:09 before the half for 17-3.

New York got it back to 13-17 — Cooper's 48-yard touchdown at Q3 10:32 and a field goal after Henderson's fumble at the NE 36 (-5.25 EPA, the game's biggest swing). The Patriots responded with a 12-play, 63-yard touchdown drive ending on Henry's 7-yarder, and when Smith threw his only interception at Q4 12:31 from a 10-point hole, the sequence was over. New England even gave the ball back again — Douglas fumbling after a 39-yard gain — and still closed it with a 12-play, 6:08 field-goal drive.

Why the game diverged from the projection

It barely did at the team level; the grade's only flagged miss is New England's 36 carries against 28.1, and that is script. The Patriots ran 62 of their 67 snaps with a lead and dropped to a 44.7% pass rate in the second half, with Kiner (11 carries, 57 yards, projected 3.7) collecting the surplus. The real divergence is inside the Jets' box score: the same 30 attempts, 33 more yards, and zero touchdowns from the three red-zone trips the model's league-constant TD split expects to produce roughly 1.7. Their field EPA was +0.076 on 52 snaps; inside the 20 it was -0.584 on nine. That is a finishing miss, not a movement miss — and their own season inputs (RZ EPA -0.216, 0-for-1 in each of weeks 4 and 5) pointed at it.

The plan against the game

The plan's shape was right and its mechanism was wrong in a specific, instructive way. It expected New England to lead and grind — it did, with the run lean confirmed at a 47.1% pass rate — and it expected the Jets' total to be capped. But it located the cap in pre-snap penalties and third downs ending drives. New York committed five flags for 35 yards with two pre-snap, against season rates of 5.2 and 2.8, and ran 61 plays at a pace the checks grade as fast (128 total snaps vs 121.5). The drives arrived; the touchdowns did not. That inversion took down the Smith yardage under, the Smith interception under (the pick came from a chasing script the plan said wouldn't materialize) and, by a point and a half, the Under. Four of ten calls hit, but the Stevenson structure calls were right for the right reasons and the Wilson miss was a role surprise — Cooper Jr., unpriced off IR, taking 7 targets for 103.

Player by player

Cooper is the headline: 82.3% of snaps, 6-103, the 48-yard score and the nullified one. Wilson fell to 6 targets and a 22.1% air-yards share, below Mason Taylor's 24.1%. Allen hit his number (17-78, four runs of 10+) but with -3.24 rush EPA and the failed fourth-down carry — chunk yardage without short-yardage reliability. On the other side, A.J. Brown's return produced 10 targets, 99 air yards and 3 catches for 27 with -7.39 EPA; Douglas (7-86-TD) and Henry (4-48-TD, 39 of it after the catch) ate what he left. Stevenson took 14 carries for 36 with three stuffs and, notably, zero targets on 60.3% of snaps.

The defenses

Charting here is box counts and rushers only — no coverage shells. The Jets blitzed on 42.9% of Maye's dropbacks (4.66 average rushers) and recorded zero sacks and four hits; Maye scrambled three times for 25 including a 21-yarder. That is the week-4 CHI profile again: pressure sent, nothing home, explosives allowed. New England's front needed less help — three sacks and six hits on 33 dropbacks (27.3%) against a 31.4% blitz rate, with Williams, Barmore and Ponder each getting one home at a drive-ending moment. New England's secondary gave up the field and held at the 20: opponents are now 0-for-3 here, 0-for-2 vs SEA, 0-for-1 vs PIT inside it.

The blueprint

To beat the Jets you do not need efficiency between the 20s — you need one or two shots over a secondary that has conceded explosive passes in four straight weeks, a quarterback who survives the blitz without a clean pocket, and enough backfield depth to take 30+ carries once ahead. To beat New England you need to finish: they hand out two turnovers a game and seven trips into their territory, and they have now watched three opponents turn that into field goals. Moving the ball on them is not the blueprint; converting is, and the Jets' own red-zone numbers said they couldn't.

What the model should take from this

Three things. The Jets' red-zone drought is now three weeks old and deserves weight the league-constant TD split won't give it — their team total will keep landing while their touchdown props keep failing. Their penalty profile should stop carrying any drive-count implication; it did not show up and it barely persists anyway. And New England's backfield should be priced as lead-dependent carries with no receiving role at all: one target to three backs over 67 snaps is not a sample artifact, it is the design.
