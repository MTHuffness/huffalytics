What happened

Pittsburgh 26, Tampa Bay 19 in overtime: the Steelers covered -2.5 and the game blew through a 38.5 total only because regulation ended 19-19 (exactly 38) and Rodgers' 6-yard touchdown to Bernard in OT added seven. The projection nailed both team totals and both rushing numbers and badly missed the volume — 149 plays, 92 pass attempts — because Tampa's offense never finished a drive and Pittsburgh spent 57 of 76 snaps trailing.

The plan read the Pittsburgh half of this game almost perfectly in shape — Rodgers threw, the yards came short and after the catch, Warren's rushing was capped, Metcalf ate the air yards — and the parlay built on that mechanism went 3-for-3. It missed the Tampa half entirely, and missed it on the mechanism, not the bounce: the plan's central premise that Tampa's front erases the run while generating no pressure got exactly inverted (Pittsburgh ran for 4.0 a carry with 3 explosives; Tampa blitzed 47.3% for 5 sacks and 10 hits), and the prescribed Tampa script of Irving at 18-22 carries with Daniels under 30 attempts collapsed into 43 attempts and 46 rushing yards. The Otton and Irving calls failed because Tampa's offense failed, which is a read miss; the Egbuka under failed while its efficiency logic held exactly (7-of-16, -11.48 EPA, both picks his way), which is a line-placement issue, not a reasoning one. The plan also correctly flagged Pittsburgh's red-zone defense as the sevens-versus-threes lever, and that is what held Tampa to 19.

The script: The favorite's path is what happened: Pittsburgh threw 49 times at a 68.4% pass rate (checks: lean called pass, actual pass, 0.711 vs a 0.647 season rate), lived on Warren-and-underneath volume plus one Metcalf conversion, converted 2 of 4 red-zone trips, and won while Tampa kicked four field goals on two red-zone trips. But the mechanism that was supposed to produce it broke twice: Pittsburgh threw because it trailed by 13, not because the run was erased (22 carries, 89 yards, 4.0 a carry, 3 explosive runs), and Tampa's 'pressure-less front' produced 5 sacks and 10 QB hits. Tampa's own lean broke outright — called run, played pass at 65.8% against a 55.3% season rate — and the pace call broke too (149 plays vs 121.5 league, in overtime).

The read

A 38.5-total game between two offenses that move the ball in opposite ways and neither of which finishes cleanly. Tampa's defense is the second-stingiest in the league in yards per carry allowed and fifth in rush EPA allowed (72.8 rush yards per game, 2nd) — it takes away the exact thing Pittsburgh wanted to lean on — but it generates the least pressure in the league (10.1%, 31st). So Pittsburgh gets a clean pocket and a dead ground game: more dropbacks than the blend assumes, yards from YAC and checkdowns, and a back whose production shifts from carries to catches. Tampa's own path is the mirror: a rookie quarterback at a 4.2–4.4 aDOT who cannot throw past the sticks, running at a Pittsburgh front that ranks 23rd in rush EPA allowed — but Pittsburgh holds at its own 20 (50% red-zone TD rate allowed, 7th), which turns Tampa drives into kicks.

Why

Pittsburgh's blended yards per carry falls to 3.6 against this front; its own season rate is 4.69. Every week that Pittsburgh has been taken out of the run it has thrown 39–40 times, and our week-4 review's lesson was explicit: price Rodgers' volume off the team's 64.7% pass rate, not the blend. Tampa has no pass rush to punish it and is without Winfield (ruled out Friday, Rotowire) with Morrison doubtful. On the other side, Tampa ran 39 times for 241 in week 5 and passed on 44.3% while ahead; Irving is the only lane and, with Tucker not expected to play (Rapoport via Rotowire, Sunday), he keeps the short-yardage work too.

The short version

Pittsburgh won 26-19 in overtime, covered -2.5, and sent a 38.5 total over by exactly the margin of the overtime touchdown — regulation finished 19-19, which is 38. The model's team totals were close to perfect (26 vs 20.5, 19 vs 18.0) and so were both rushing lines; what it could not see was 149 plays, 92 pass attempts and a game in which one team's quarterback threw for 164 yards on 43 attempts and the other threw for 253 on 49 with no completion longer than 22.

How it unfolded

Tampa built its lead on Pittsburgh's mistakes and a flag. Rodgers was intercepted by A'Shawn Robinson on the Steelers' second possession (Q1 10:51, -5.32 EPA), handing the Bucs the ball at the PIT 39 and three points. Everette's 22-yard DPI at the start of the second quarter put Tampa in scoring range on the drive that ended with Daniels' 2-yard touchdown to Egbuka at 14:55 — free yards that never show in the box score. By 4:12 of the second it was 13-0, and a Boswell 52-yarder as the half expired was all Pittsburgh had for 30 minutes of football.

Tampa's third quarter is the game in miniature: a 16-play, 70-yard, 9:02 drive that produced three points, 16-3. That is a two-possession lead that should have been three. Pittsburgh then went 9 plays and 53 yards for a touchdown (Washington, Q4 13:53), Everette intercepted Daniels 34 seconds later, and the Steelers ground back in field goals — a 39-yarder, then a 58-yarder at the two-minute warning to tie. Jamel Dean's interception at Q4 1:46, returned 40 yards to the TB 3 (-6.89 EPA, -0.45 WPA), looked decisive until McLaughlin answered from 53 with 13 seconds left. In overtime Pittsburgh went 12 plays and 70 yards in 6:45, rescued on 3rd-and-13 by a 20-yard Metcalf crosser, and Bernard caught the winner from six yards.

Why the game diverged from the projection

Two mechanisms, both volume. Pittsburgh spent 57 of 76 snaps trailing and threw 70.2% of the time in that state against an engine blend of 58.7%; overtime added a dozen more plays. Tampa's divergence is more interesting and more useful: it led for 3,055 game-seconds and passed 68.1% of 47 snaps while ahead, because Irving was being buried (19 carries, 46 yards, 5 stuffs, -6.28 rush EPA, and 4 middle runs for -4 yards). The engine's 55.1% blended pass rate was a fair read of Tampa's season; the game it was handed had no running game in it.

The scoring projections survived all of that because the efficiency inputs were right. Pittsburgh's field EPA was -0.184 and Tampa's -0.277; neither offense could sustain. Eight field goals and two touchdowns is what that produces.

The plan against the game

The plan's Pittsburgh half was close to a clean read — pass lean confirmed (71.1% dropback rate vs a 64.7% season rate), Warren's rushing capped, Metcalf fed, the yards from YAC, the points from converted trips — and the three-leg parlay built on it cashed in full. The Tampa half inverted. The premise was that Tampa's front erases the run while never touching the quarterback; instead Pittsburgh ran for 4.0 a carry with three explosive runs, and Tampa blitzed 47.3% of Rodgers' dropbacks for five sacks and ten hits. That is why Irving and Otton missed: not variance, a mis-sorted matchup. The Egbuka under is the opposite case — the efficiency logic held exactly (7-of-16, -11.48 EPA, both picks thrown his way), and 16 targets beat it anyway.

Player by player

Metcalf and Bernard were the game. Twenty-seven targets, 22 catches, 180 yards and the winning touchdown between them, against 12.1 projected targets. With Pittman, Wilson and Skowronek unavailable there was nowhere else for the ball to go, and Rodgers' 4.1 aDOT turned that concentration into a catch-and-run market: 152 team YAC, zero completions of 20+ yards. Warren got the carries (14) and almost none of the routes (4 targets, 4 air yards), which is the inverse of what a 52-dropback game normally yields and the single most instructive prop result here. Dowdle, back from the toe, out-produced him per touch (7-40, two 10+ runs).

On Tampa's side Otton played every snap for two targets at negative air yards. Egbuka got 16 targets and -11.48 EPA. Daniels' -15.0 cpoe is the number to carry: 22-of-43 at a 6.3 aDOT, with the four scrambles (39 yards, including a 4th-and-9 conversion in overtime) the only part of his line that threatened anybody.

The defenses

Charting for pressure and coverage has not landed, so read the proxies. Pittsburgh's pass rush produced 5 sacks and 9 hits at a modest 26.4% opponent blitz rate — Ramsey had two sacks and four TFL, Herbig two sacks and three TFL, with Heyward forcing the Everette interception. That is a front getting home without help, which is more than the season rate (14% pressure) suggested. Tampa's rush was the opposite: it needed a 47.3% blitz rate to collect five sacks, which worked against a quarterback with zero scrambles but left 33 completions underneath.

In the red zone Pittsburgh was again the bend-don't-break unit tagged in week 5: Tampa made two trips in 74 plays and scored once. But the binding constraint on Tampa was earlier — it never got there.

What the model should take from this

The play-count input is the weak link, not the efficiency inputs. The same projection that missed both attempt lines by double digits got both team totals, both rushing yardages and the side and spread correct. Where plays are projected below 61 for a favorite the model expects to lead, discount the volume projections and keep the scoring ones.

Second, target distribution with a decimated receiver room needs a stronger prior. The engine gave Bernard 5.0 targets and he saw 12 on a 93.7% snap share; Metcalf's 7.1 became 15. The air-yards share (68.6% for Metcalf) is the kind of concentration that should be forecastable from the inactive list alone.

Third: 'no pass rush' as a defensive tag has a mobility condition on it. And finally, the Under on a 38.5 total was alive for 60 minutes. In low-total games between two sub-zero field-EPA offenses, the overtime tail is a live share of the loss distribution.
