The read

Carolina lays three on the road in a game the market prices at 39.5 because Philadelphia's offense has been dismantled: Jalen Hurts is on injured reserve (Rotowire, Sat), Saquon Barkley is out and Shipley and Bigsby are on IR, and the line that started Week 3 has lost Lane Johnson and Cam Jurgens outright with Dickerson ruled out late Saturday. That points at a Carolina-leads script: Hubbard carries, clock, and Young throwing fewer than his four-game norm. Philadelphia's path is volume from behind — McKee into a Carolina front that has not generated pressure all season.

Why

Carolina's defense is 32nd in pressure generated and 32nd in yards per carry allowed, and every prior review says the same thing: a clean pocket makes even a replacement quarterback functional (Chicago's backup went 0 sacks, 27 points in Week 3 against this same Philadelphia team's problem, and Detroit threw for 412 on Carolina in Week 4). That keeps McKee's passing floor higher than a debut usually warrants, and Carolina's 27th-ranked red-zone touchdown rate allowed means Philadelphia's few trips should finish.

Going the other way, Philadelphia bends between the 20s (22nd in field EPA allowed) but holds at the goal line (8th in red-zone touchdown rate allowed). That is the case for Carolina yardage over Carolina touchdowns — Young moving it, Hubbard eating carries, and more field goals than a 21-point implied total suggests. Jalen Coker's return from a quad also pulls Carolina's target tree back toward where it was in Weeks 1-2, which caps the McMillan-takes-everything shape of Week 4.

What would change it

Philadelphia scoring first flips the run/pass leans instantly — our Week 4 review of Carolina was wrong on exactly that, assuming a chase script that never came. Carolina's own tackle situation (Moton activated but questionable, Freeling doubtful) could put Young under real pressure for the first time against a front with Carter and Greenard. And McKee has no body of work: one bad quarter and Philadelphia's passing lines are dead.

The short version

Carolina won 25-14 as a 3-point road favorite and the game landed a half-point under a 39.5 total — three for three on the model's market calls. The margin came from a possession game Philadelphia had no tools to contest: the Eagles ran the ball 13 times for 35 yards, Carolina ran it 33 times for 126 and held the ball for 34:48, and Chuba Hubbard turned 28 carries into 120 yards and a touchdown. Nobody turned the ball over. Carolina scored three touchdowns on four red-zone trips; Philadelphia only reached the red zone twice all afternoon.

How it unfolded

The game's tone was set on the second snap, when Bobby Okereke dropped Tanner McKee for an 11-yard loss and Philadelphia punted from its own 15. Carolina took over at its own 39 and kicked a 47-yarder. Over the first quarter and a half the Panthers punted four times without ever losing the field-position argument, and when a short field finally arrived — Philadelphia's punt pinned at the CAR 35 following a three-and-out — Bryce Young found Tommy Tremble for 12 and a 10-0 lead at Q2 8:14, with a roughing-the-passer flag on a 16-yard McMillan completion doing the heavy lifting on the drive.

Philadelphia's only sustained drive of the half came next: 11 plays, 61 yards, 5:26, finished by Dameon Pierce from two yards out. Carolina answered the threat of a halftime swing on 3rd-and-15 from its own 15, when Young went deep left to Tetairoa McMillan for 21 — the biggest win-probability play of the game at +0.113.

The third quarter is where it was decided. Carolina took the second-half kickoff and milked 3:28 off the clock for a 52-yard field goal, then, after a Philadelphia punt pinned them at their own 8, put together 14 plays and 94 yards over 7:43. Jalen Coker's 32-yard catch on 3rd-and-8 from the 10 (+3.32 EPA) was the first conversion; Hubbard's 19-yard run on 4th-and-1 at the PHI 31 (+2.89) was the last. Hubbard punched it in at Q4 13:39 for 19-7.

Philadelphia's one real push followed — a 10-play, 69-yard no-huddle drive capped by a 19-yard McKee-to-Smith touchdown at Q4 10:48. They got the ball back at their own 48 with 9:32 left, and a botched exchange between Majors and McKee at the 43 (-1.52 EPA) killed it. Four plays later Young hit Tremble for 19 and the game.

Why the game diverged from the projection

The grade is a B carried by the market side. The big misses are all Philadelphia volume: 26.6 projected carries became 13, 116.6 projected rush yards became 35, 32.6 projected attempts became 45. The mechanism is script plus personnel, and it was recognizable. Philadelphia never led for a second and never had more than one back on the roster worth handing to — Barkley, Shipley and Bigsby were all unavailable. The engine blended the Eagles' 3.785 YPC offense against Carolina's 5.397 allowed and arrived at a perfectly reasonable 4.378; it just never got the attempts to express it.

Carolina's miss runs the other direction. Its pass rate fell to 55.4% from a 64.6% season figure, right in line with the blended 58%, but the team ran 76 plays against a projected 63.9 because it controlled the clock. That is why Young still threw 39 times for 281 while Hubbard got 28 carries: possession count, not a chase script.

The plan against the game

The plan's favorite path reads like a transcript — Carolina scores early, feeds Hubbard, Philadelphia chases with 35-plus dropbacks, the Panthers kick field goals, mid-20s to mid-teens. Both lean calls held and the two pillars (Hubbard over, McKee passing over) came in. Two things it got wrong matter. It assumed Pierce's default 20-plus carries; the Eagles abandoned the run entirely, going 85.7% pass in the second half, and he finished with 12. And it assumed neither quarterback would be touched — Carolina put 10 hits on McKee behind a line missing Dickerson, something the plan's own unknowns list had flagged as the biggest threat and then didn't price. The two Coker unders were the costliest misses and they were a role read, not a bounce: no ramp, 74.7% of snaps, team-high targets.

Player by player

Hubbard is the clean one: lead-script bell cow, 93.7% snaps, +5.09 rush EPA, three 10-plus runs, 15 of 28 carries to the left side at 4.9 a pop. Coker is the lesson: back from a quad and immediately the air-yards leader at 42%, 13 targets, 97 yards. McMillan held his catch floor (6 of 9 for 86) while ceding air yards exactly as expected. Tremble's two touchdowns on four targets are variance, but his 68.4% snap share against Darren Waller's 45.6% is not — Waller is a rotational piece and the projection's 4.2 targets for him was too generous.

On the other side, DeVonta Smith's 14 targets are real volume with poor yield (-0.49 receiving EPA, 75 yards). Dallas Goedert was the Eagles' most productive player (+4.36 EPA, 61 yards on 8 looks) precisely because a pressured quarterback with no receiving back checks down. McKee's line — 26/45, 215, 1-for-7 deep, -6.4 cpoe — is the archetype of volume without efficiency.

The defenses

Charting is snap counts only this week; coverage and pressure charting hasn't landed, so read the proxies. Carolina's front, tagged 'no pass rush' twice this season, generated a 20.8% sack-or-hit rate against a Philadelphia interior missing Dickerson, including Lloyd's sack at Q3 7:15 and the Moehrig/Phillips split at Q4 5:31. Philadelphia's front, tagged three times, got two sacks and four hits on 43 Young dropbacks. The Eagles blitzed at 37.3% with a 6.06 average box and got neither pressure nor run stops. Carolina's secondary gave up 45 DPI yards on two Will Lee calls but allowed zero explosive passes — the first time all season an opponent managed none against them.

The blueprint

Philadelphia's is the loud one and it's four weeks deep: negative field EPA every game since Week 3, a run game at -0.42 and -0.61 EPA/rush the prior two weeks, and now 2.7 yards a carry. Any opponent with league-average rush efficiency and a lead will do to them what Carolina did, because they have no counter-punch. Carolina's is subtler — it cannot rush the passer and gives up explosives (five to CHI, four to DET), but exploiting it requires a quarterback who can actually complete the deep ball behind a line that holds. Philadelphia tried the blueprint and had none of the ingredients.

What the model should take from this

Three carries. First, when an underdog's entire backfield is out, halve the team carry projection rather than redistributing the share — the pass attempts will inflate but the pass yards usually won't. Second, 'no pass rush' on a defense is conditional on the opposing line; with a guard and center compromised, take the sack-and-hit over anyway. Third, a returning receiver with a competing injury absence takes the full role immediately; the ramp assumption cost two calls and a third of the player grade.
