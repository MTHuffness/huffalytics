Houston won 34-31 as a 2.5-point dog and blew the 42.5 total out by 22.5 points: neither defense generated a single sack, both quarterbacks topped 300 yards, and the game turned on a Stroud red-zone interception that only bought Jacksonville a 60-yard field goal before Fairbairn's 49-yarder at 0:03.

The plan correctly identified the mechanism that produced the points — two secondaries that concede yardage and two fronts that could not get home — and it correctly picked the underdog's winners (Stroud over 300, Collins over, the explosive-play battle). The read

The short version

Houston won 34-31 at Wembley as a 2.5-point underdog, and the 42.5 total was never in the conversation: 65 points, 127 plays, 618 passing yards between the two quarterbacks and not one sack in 78 dropbacks. The projection had 20-22.5 and a one-score grind; it got the volume almost exactly right (HOU 34 attempts and 25 carries against 33.8 and 24.5) and the efficiency almost entirely wrong. Houston never trailed by more than four, led for 2,699 of 3,600 seconds, survived its own goal-line interception with four minutes left, and won on Ka'imi Fairbairn's 49-yarder at 0:03 after Cam Little had tied it from 60.

How it unfolded

Jacksonville's first three possessions produced a punt, a failed fourth-and-3 at the Houston 37 (Q1 8:21, -2.35 EPA), and — after a 15-play, 69-yard, 6:55 march — a Trevor Lawrence interception at the goal line by Calen Bullock at Q2 13:24, the second-biggest swing of the day at -4.82 EPA. That is 25 plays and zero points from the team the market made favorite. Houston, meanwhile, needed five plays and 62 yards to score, Stroud's 42-yarder to Collins at Q1 6:50 doing most of it.

The second quarter turned into a shootout nobody projected: Tuten's 27-yard catch-and-run TD, a Fairbairn 25-yarder, Tuten again from two, then Houston's four-play, 51-yard answer in 47 seconds built on Noel's 50-yard catch down the middle — 17-14 at the break. Houston opened the third with an 11-play, 65-yard drive capped by Noel's 13-yard touchdown on third-and-10, and after Jacksonville cut it to three, the Texans drew 19 DPI yards from Jarrian Jones at Q3 1:02 and immediately ran Jawhar Jordan 25 yards off left tackle for the touchdown that made it 31-21.

Jacksonville got back within three on Travis Hunter's seven-yard score at Q4 11:30, Houston ate 7:33 off the clock on a 13-play drive, and then Stroud threw the game's biggest play backwards — an end-zone interception to Hunter from the four-yard line at Q4 3:59, -6.02 EPA and -0.241 WPA. Jacksonville moved 38 yards and kicked from 60. Houston answered with Marks' 19-yard catch on third-and-2 and the winning kick.

Why the game diverged from the projection

Two inputs failed. The first is pressure: the engine blended sack rates of 6.2% and 7.1% and projected better than two sacks a side. Actual pressure (sack or hit) came in at 5.7% for Houston's rush and 7.0% for Jacksonville's, roughly a third of the 15.9% league rate, and both quarterbacks played clean — Stroud at 0.373 EPA per dropback with 11.5 CPOE, Lawrence at 0.268 despite -5.0 CPOE because his receivers produced 153 yards after catch. The second is the run matchup. Jacksonville's 2.93 YPC allowed was the keystone of the pre-game read; Houston ran 25 times for 114 (4.56), with 65 of those yards on 11 left-side carries.

Jacksonville's one big volume miss — 41 attempts against 31.5 — is game state and nothing else: 58 of 69 snaps trailing, a 0.621 pass rate in that state. That is noise the model should not chase. Houston's point total is mixed: the yardage was earned, but 3 touchdowns on 5 red-zone trips against a defense the engine shrunk to .572 was the small-sample coin landing heads.

The plan against the game

The plan's headline mechanism — two yardage-conceding secondaries, neither front getting home — is exactly the game that was played, and its underdog path named the winners correctly down to "Collins and the complementary receivers." But it got there backwards. It assumed Jacksonville's front would dictate and Jacksonville would hold the lead; instead Houston scored first, ran the ball well, and threw only 34 times, which is why both lean checks failed in opposite directions and why the Marks unders and the Hutchinson yardage over all lost on the same broken premise. Pace was called perfectly.

Player by player

Noel is the headline and the caution: 4-for-4, 107 yards and a touchdown on 49% of snaps, with 61 air yards and 46 YAC. Two explosives on four targets is not a repeatable projection, but a 23.6% air-yards share at half the snaps is a role worth tracking. Collins remains the alpha without the target volume — 5 looks, 73 air yards, plus 19 DPI yards drawn that never appear in his line. Schultz led the team in targets with six, which is the direct refutation of the pre-game read on him. Hutchinson collapsed to three targets with Harrison Jr. drawing four on 19% of snaps.

For Jacksonville, the backfield split is the actionable item: Tuten 57.7% of snaps, Chris Rodriguez 29.6% and 11 carries for 55 — more productive per carry than the starter. Tuten's value was entirely receiving (4 catches, 48 yards, two touchdowns, -13 air yards). Washington carried 40.7% of the air yards for four catches on eleven targets; that is a volume play whose yardage lives or dies on deep completion rate.

The defenses

Charting here is box and blitz only — no coverage shells. Jacksonville blitzed 41% and rushed 4.59 on average without producing a hit that mattered; Travon Walker played 79% of snaps for nothing in the box score. Houston blitzed 37% at an average of 4.49 rushers and also recorded zero sacks. Both defenses defended the field badly (HOU allowed 0.192 EPA/play between the 20s, JAX 0.308) and neither got the compensating takeaway volume beyond one each. Houston's secondary has now allowed 20-plus-yard passes in six straight games; Jacksonville's corner room, missing Montaric Brown with Jaylon Jones limited, gave up two 40-plus completions and 36 DPI yards.

What the model should take from this

The engine's volume machinery is working. Its efficiency environment is not sensitive enough to pass-rush quality. Two teams with recurring "no pass rush" tags and recurring explosive-pass allowances met, and the blend still produced a 42.5-equivalent number. That is a total-side edge sitting in the season-context tags. Second, the red-zone constant did what the dossier says it does — nothing predictive — so the finishing swing should not be re-weighted off nine trips. Third, Houston's 62.6% season pass rate is a chase-game artifact; its leading-state rate is 52.6%, and that distinction should enter the lean before any Texans prop is written.
