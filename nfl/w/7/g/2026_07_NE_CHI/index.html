The full story · the report's account, start to finish

The short version

Chicago never trailed, never turned it over, and won 30-24 as a 3-point favorite with the total sailing over 50.5. The model's scoreboard work was close to perfect — 23.8 projected for New England against 24 scored, 26.8 for Chicago against 30, pass attempts within a single throw for both quarterbacks — but the game it priced and the game that happened shared almost no internal structure. The projection imagined 54 combined carries and 464 passing yards; the actual split was 47 carries and 629 passing yards, with eight Chicago explosive passes against a league average of three.

How it unfolded

New England's first possession reached the Chicago 46 and died there on a fourth-and-5 sack by Kyler Gordon (Q1 11:35, EPA -2.88), gifting the Bears the ball at midfield for Santos's 43-yard field goal. Chicago's second scoring drive needed one play: Burden behind Kevin Byard for 58 to the 4 at Q1 2:32, Swift in from four yards at 1:43, 10-0. New England answered with a 9-play, 68-yard march capped by Maye's scramble to the 1 and a goal-line score, but the second quarter was where the game got decided. Trailing 13-7 with a drive at the Chicago 15, Maye threw for Raridon into the end zone and D'Marco Jackson intercepted it (Q2 3:32, EPA -5.27). Chicago's answer drive was twice stopped and twice extended — a defensive holding on Woods at 2:58, Gabe Jacas's 15-yard roughing on a 22-yard Burden completion at 2:21 — and ended with Burden's 24-yard touchdown on 3rd-and-17 at 0:21. Maye's 48-yard throw to Stevenson with three seconds left (EPA +6.54) made it 20-14 instead of 20-7.

The second half was Chicago bleeding clock and New England chasing. The Bears' 10-play, 82-yard opening drive produced Loveland's 12-yard score; the Patriots answered with their best possession of the night, 10 plays and 90 yards, Raridon 30 yards for the touchdown at Q3 3:52 (WPA +0.106). From there Chicago ran 13 plays over 6:56 (missed 53-yard field goal), then 11 more over 4:48 ending in Santos's 58-yarder at Q4 1:39 — the kick that made the last New England drive academic.

Why the game diverged from the projection

Two mechanisms. First, game state: Chicago led for 3,298 of 3,600 seconds, so New England ran 45 of its 52 snaps trailing and threw at a 67.3% rate against a 55.5% blend. That alone explains the 9-carry miss. But it is not the whole story — the Patriots' 15 designed runs produced -0.404 EPA per rush and a 20% success rate, continuing a season in which their rush EPA has been negative in every single game. The soft-front argument was always a matchup abstraction that their own numbers refused.

Second, efficiency over the top. The engine blended 7.07 and 7.68 yards per attempt; the game produced 9.3 and 10.4. Chicago went 5-for-8 on deep throws for 144 yards against a secondary without Carlton Davis, Prunty and Canada, and the charting shows New England answered by blitzing on 61% of dropbacks against a quarterback whose season EPA vs the blitz is +0.176. Three sacks came out of that; so did 0.396 EPA per dropback.

The plan against the game

The plan's favorite path reads like a transcript of Chicago's night — 68 snaps, time of possession, Swift grinding, Maye giving one away — and it still went 1-9 on calls, because both of its levers were wrong in the same direction. It asked New England to run (actual pass rate 0.66) and asked Chicago to stall inside the 20 (3 touchdowns on 4 trips). The pace call went the other way too: 116 plays against a 121.5 league pair is average, so the over cashed on yards per play rather than snaps. The two calls whose reasoning survived were the ones built on role rather than efficiency — Swift's checkdown volume and Brown's targets-before-production — and both still landed on the wrong side of their numbers by small margins.

Player by player

The distribution misses, not the volume misses, sank the player score (0.358, 29 of 50 lines). Loveland saw 12 targets and 47.5% of Chicago's air yards against a projection of 5.6; Hunter Henry caught all seven of his against a 4.1 projection; Stevenson's value was 93 receiving yards on five targets, not 22 rushing on six carries. A.J. Brown played 90.7% of snaps and carried 149 air yards (52.8% share) into three catches for 48 — high-volume, low-conversion, exactly his week-6 shape. Burden's 4-for-130 is the line to ignore: 32.5 yards per target on 60.9% of snaps is a bounce, and Odunze (94.2% snaps, five targets, three fourth-quarter 21-yarders) is the more stable piece of that room. Kalif Raymond played 69.6% of snaps for two targets and nothing — a usage-share artifact the engine keeps paying.

The defenses

New England's front did its job: Chicago gained 116 yards on 30 carries at 3.87 per attempt with -0.125 EPA per rush, and Barmore, Leonard Taylor and Marcus Jones each got a sack. The breakdown was behind them. Cameron Lewis allowed 3-of-3 for 74 and a touchdown, Devin Bush's counterpart on the other side — New England's Marcus Jones — missed three tackles, and Byard gave up 64 on three targets. Chicago's defense was the mirror image: Gordon's fourth-down sack and Jackson's end-zone interception were the two plays that mattered, while Maye completed 21 of 24 short throws for 257 yards and +15.3 CPOE against a 37.8% blitz rate.

The blueprint

To beat New England you throw past it and don't give it anything back — that is four of seven weeks now with explosive-pass damage on the dropback-EPA line while the field numbers stay respectable. To beat Chicago you need a four-man rush that gets home; this week's opponent instead sent five-plus on 61% of snaps without the corners to support it, and conceded eight 20-plus plays doing so. Chicago's famous red-zone problem and turnover problem both simply did not appear, which is the honest caveat: those are the least persistent items in the file.

What the model should take from this

One real read: New England's carry projection should be built off its trailing-state rate and its own rush efficiency, not off the opponent's run-defense rank. One real read on the other side: Loveland's target share is structural, Raymond's snap share is not. And one thing to leave alone — the red-zone finishing swing that turned a correct 3-point projected margin into a 6-point loss is noise, and the engine was right to keep the touchdown split on the league constant.

Traps · where a line can fool you next time

Do not project a run script for a team whose own rush EPA is negative every week, no matter how soft the opposing front ranks. New England was supposed to run 26-30 times at the league's worst rush-EPA defense and ran 15 for 70 yards.

Trigger: Offense with rush EPA below -0.10 in 5+ games and an opponent favored by 3 or more — fade the RB rushing overs and take the QB passing over instead.

Totals

Red-zone tags are the least persistent thing in the file. Chicago, tagged for red-zone stalls three times in six weeks, went 3-for-4 against a defense that had allowed 0 TDs on 3 trips the prior week.

Trigger: Any 'they stall inside the 20' vs 'bend-don't-break' pairing — price the total off field EPA and drive count, not off red-zone TD rate.

Props

A 4-target, 130-yard receiving line is a bounce, not a role. Burden's night was one 58-yard shot and a 24-yard TD on 3rd-and-17; his snap share was 60.9% and Loveland (12 targets) is the actual volume.

Trigger: When a WR's receiving yards exceed 25 per target in one game, buy the opponent's under the following week unless target share also moved.

Heavy blitz against a quarterback with positive EPA vs the blitz is an over signal. New England blitzed 61% and surrendered 0.396 EPA/dropback and 8 explosives.

Trigger: Defense blitz rate above 40% + opposing QB season EPA vs blitz above 0.15 + two or more starting corners out.

Trigger: Underdog of 3+ with below-median rush efficiency: shade RB carries down 20% and QB attempts/receiving yards up.
