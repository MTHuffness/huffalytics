The full story · the report's account, start to finish

The short version

Cleveland won 27-24 on a 32-yard kick with three seconds left, covering -1.5 and sending a 36.5-point total over by fourteen and a half. The number that explains the whole afternoon: six rushing touchdowns, zero passing touchdowns, in a game both the market and the model had framed as two stalled offenses trading field goals. Cleveland put up 70 plays, 491 yards and converted three of four red-zone trips against a defense rated third in the league inside the 20; Tennessee needed 17 red-zone plays to score twice and still lost by three.

How it unfolded

Tennessee opened in no-huddle and Pollard went 36 yards off right tackle on the sixth snap. Watson's second-quarter-of-the-first-quarter throw for Boston was picked by Flott and returned to the CLE 24 (Q1 10:47, -5.32 EPA), and four plays later Pollard was in again from 10. Down 14-0, Cleveland answered with a 73-yard drive built on a 23-yard Concepcion catch and a 30-yard Judkins run to the TEN 5.

The second quarter was the hinge. Tennessee spent 14 plays and 7:56 getting to the CLE 1, took a sack on 3rd-and-1 (Q2 10:24) and watched Pollard get stuffed by Reid on 4th-and-1 (Q2 9:39, -3.42 EPA). Cleveland started at its own 1, Fannin was stripped at the CLE 40, and Tennessee gave the field position straight back when Ward's deep ball for Ayomanor was intercepted at the CLE 22 six seconds later. Cleveland drove 73 to tie, then 64 more in 1:45 to kick a 28-yarder at 0:04 for a 17-14 halftime lead.

The third quarter produced nothing but a Tate fumble and a missed 43-yard field goal to open the fourth. Slye tied it at 8:44, Cleveland answered with eight plays and 71 yards — Concepcion's 18-yard end-around to the 9, Watson's 9-yard keeper — and Tennessee came back with an 11-play touchdown drive that lived on a 26-yard 4th-and-8 conversion to Ayomanor (+4.4 EPA) and an 18-yard throw to Tate at the CLE 1 on 3rd-and-12. Pollard scored at 1:01. Cleveland needed three plays and 30 seconds: Fannin for 36 to the TEN 19, then a Tennessee offside in field-goal formation that shortened the winner to 32 yards.

Why the game diverged from the projection

The engine had the margin (projected -1.5, actual -3) and missed the level. Two mechanisms: pace and Cleveland's efficiency. The blend gave Cleveland 61.4 plays when its opponents had been running 65.5 against it; the game ran 128 combined snaps against a 121.5 league average. Then Cleveland ran it for 183 at 6.3 a carry — 118 yards on 13 left-side runs — after six straight weeks of negative rush EPA and league-worst yardage before contact. And the finishing flipped: Cleveland's four trips produced +0.611 EPA per red-zone play against a defense that had allowed 46.2% touchdowns per trip. Those are the grade's two big flags (pass yards +90.5, rush yards +74.9) and only one of them is a read the model should keep.

The plan against the game

The plan saw the right skeleton — a one-score game decided in the final four minutes by a kicker, Cleveland holding the ball, Pollard carrying the Titans — and inverted the two mechanisms that produced the points. It sold the red-zone wall and watched Cleveland score touchdowns on three of four trips; it called Cleveland's run game broken on six weeks of evidence and watched Judkins go for 96. Its Watson passing over (308) landed for precisely the stated reason: no pass rush, quick game, 24-of-30 behind the sticks. Its downgrades on Judkins, Concepcion and Fannin all failed on the same miscount — a 70-play game is not the 62-snap, 30-throw pie the call was built on.

Player by player

Concepcion was the game: 10 targets, 8-129, 82 YAC, three carries for 23 including the run that set up the winning touchdown. Fannin caught six for 91 on nine targets with the two biggest third-down and clock-drive chunks, and also lost the strip at the CLE 40. Boston is the cautionary line — 87.1% of snaps, 8 targets, 134 air yards, 46.2% of the air-yards share, and three catches for 27 because Cleveland went 1-of-6 beyond the sticks. On the other side, Pollard's 25-129-3 masked -2.65 rush EPA and five stuffs: the workload is real (Spears inactive, Carter at three carries), the efficiency was two first-quarter explosions. Ayomanor, not Tate, led Tennessee in air-yards share with Ridley out.

The defenses

Neither pass rush existed in a way that mattered: one sack apiece, 6 QB hits for Tennessee on 38 dropbacks, 4 for Cleveland on 33. Tennessee's charted blitz rate entering was 24.6% and Cleveland faced 22% with an average 4.29 rushers — pressure charting hasn't landed for this game, so the proxies are the sack and hit rates, and they say both quarterbacks were comfortable. Cleveland's secondary did its damage on the ball: Denzel Ward played through the hand for three breakups and the interception, and Hickman recovered the Tate fumble. Tennessee's front got the one play it needed — Reid's stuff at the goal line was Cleveland's, actually Reid is Cleveland's; Tennessee's equivalent was Hill punching the ball out of Fannin.

The blueprint

Tennessee is the clearer of the two: explosive passing allowed in weeks 1, 4 and 5, no pass rush in 1 and 5, and six 20-plus pass plays here, all short throws with YAC. You do not need arm talent to beat them — you need completion percentage at a 7-ish aDOT and two receivers who run after the catch. Cleveland gets beaten by the run and by its own giveaways near midfield; Tennessee did both and still lost, because 5-of-13 on third down and 17 red-zone plays for two touchdowns is not a closing kit.

What the model should take from this

Three things. Use the defense's plays-allowed when two small offenses meet — the pace blend cost ten points of total. Treat six-game red-zone ranks as decoration, not input; the engine's own note says the rate barely persists, and this game is why. And do not rewrite Cleveland's run model off one afternoon: the carry count was right, the 6.3 a carry is the observation to log and discount.
