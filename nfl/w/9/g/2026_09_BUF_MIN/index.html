The full story · the report's account, start to finish

The short version

Buffalo covered -3 and the under cashed by 16.5 points, and neither was close after halftime. The projection had Buffalo at 24.2 and the Bills scored 23; it had Minnesota at 21.2 and the Vikings scored 6. The gap is entirely the home offense: 47 snaps, 0-for-9 on third down, 0-for-2 on fourth, three giveaways and 81 passing yards from Carson Wentz before he was replaced. Buffalo did not play well — -0.038 EPA/play, 3.05 yards a carry, four sacks allowed, eight penalties — it simply never had to.

How it unfolded

Dortch's 58-yard kickoff return to open the game (EPA +2.60) was the biggest win-probability play of the night and the shape of everything after: Buffalo started at the MIN 36 and Minnesota spent the evening in its own end. The first quarter traded disasters — Allen's 3rd-and-23 heave to Moore picked by Byron Murphy and returned 54 yards (EPA -4.66), then one snap later Wentz's aborted-snap fumble, reversed into a turnover on a Buffalo challenge (EPA -6.01). Bass kicked a 39-yarder at Q1 02:00; Buffalo led from there to the gun.

The only touchdown of the first three quarters was Allen's 25-yard throw to Latu at Q2 00:53, capping an 8-play, 80-yard drive that turned 3-3 into 10-3. Minnesota's best moment came immediately after: a 51-yard, 39-second drive to a Reichard 53-yarder. Then the third quarter settled it. A 19-yard DPI on Bernard (Q3 13:26) handed the Vikings free yards into Buffalo territory; Wentz threw an interception to Dorian Williams at the BUF 11 two snaps later. Buffalo answered with 14 plays and 7:08. At Q3 04:10 Wentz threw his second pick, to Bernard. Buffalo then ran 15 plays and 8:32, keeping the drive alive with Allen's reviewed 4th-and-1 sneak at the MIN 38, and Cook finished it. Final possession math: Buffalo 40:33 and 76 snaps, Minnesota 17:28 and 47.

Why the game diverged from the projection

The volume model was right and the efficiency model was wrong. Minnesota's 32 pass attempts matched projection exactly — in 14 fewer plays than projected, because trailing all night pushed the pass rate to 0.721 and the carries to 12 against 26.9 projected. Buffalo's 44 carries against 27.8 is the mirror image: the blend used a 0.539 pass rate for a team whose season rate was 0.501 and whose week-8 answer with a lead was 41 handoffs. That's a signal the engine should carry forward, not noise.

What the engine could not price was -20.0 team CPOE. Minnesota's inputs already said this offense was below league between the 20s (-0.086 field EPA, 6.73 YPA); they did not say its quarterback would complete eight of twenty-five. The one input that pointed at it: Buffalo blitzes at 0.722 and Minnesota's offense sat at -0.197 EPA vs the blitz, the worst intersection on the sheet.

The plan against the game

The plan wrote the favorite path and the game ran it line for line — Buffalo scores first, hands it to Cook, converts a fourth down, plays 70-plus snaps, Bass kicks three times, Bills by a touchdown-plus in the low-to-mid 20s. The checks back it: pace average as called, Buffalo's lean 'run' at 0.443 against a 0.501 season rate. Where it was conservative was Minnesota's floor — it planned for a low-volume night with points in threes and got a shutout-adjacent collapse — and in Cook's carry range, which it set at 18-22 and the game delivered at 31. Its Minnesota 'balanced' lean broke for the only reason a lean ever breaks: the team never led a second.

Player by player

Cook's 107 yards arrived on 31 carries with -3.72 rushing EPA, two stuffs and one run of 10+. The over cashed; the mechanism (explosive-run leakage) did not. Allen went over 224.5 with 234 on three explosives — the 36-yard third-and-8 to Ty Johnson, the 25-yard TD, a 22-yarder to Shakir — in a game where he was hit ten times and sacked four. Knox is the quiet note: 72.5% snaps, five targets, 37 yards against a 2.3-target projection, the heavy-personnel beneficiary of a grind script. Moore carried 38.7% of the air yards for 25 receiving yards and the interception.

On the other side, Jefferson's role was exactly as modeled — 97.9% snaps, 11 targets, 138 air yards — and produced three catches. Hockenson got the seven targets the plan expected and 33 yards. Jones's 5 carries on 35.4% of snaps behind Mason's 47.9% is the actionable one: the engine's backfield hierarchy is stale.

The defenses

Charting isn't published here, so read the proxies. Minnesota's front was the better unit: 25.3% pressure entering, 27.8% sack-or-hit delivered, four sacks of -7, -5, -8 (strip-sack recovered at the BUF 1 before reversal) and -9, with Dawkins absent and Grable at every tackle snap. It also did the thing its season inputs promised — 0.286 red-zone TD rate allowed became 3 Buffalo trips, 1 touchdown, -0.218 EPA inside the 20. Buffalo's defense simply harvested a bad quarterback: 9 QB hits, 27.3% pressure, two interceptions, 1 explosive pass allowed after conceding 4-5 in six straight weeks.

The blueprint

Buffalo's weakness — chunk passing and red-zone touchdowns allowed — is real and recurring, and completely unavailable to an offense that goes 1-of-6 deep. That is the trap to carry: the exploit requires the exploiter. Minnesota's weakness — third down, league-low volume, an offense that cannot survive a deficit — was cashable by any team willing to grind, and Buffalo cashed it without running the ball well at all.

What the model should take from this

Three things. Model Buffalo's lead-state pass rate, not the blend: two straight games at 41 and 44 carries. Cut the Minnesota backfield's Jones share and the team's rush volume in any trailing projection. And separate role from efficiency in prop work — the plan's role-driven calls went 8-for-11 while two blended carry projections scored zero.
