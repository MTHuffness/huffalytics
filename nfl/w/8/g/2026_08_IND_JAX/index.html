more

Why

The chain is simple: Jacksonville stuffs the run, Indianapolis throws 35-plus, Jones's attempts and his giveaway risk both rise (an interception in six of seven games, three turnovers twice), Jacksonville plays with a lead and runs.

Indianapolis is 7th-worst at turning trips into touchdowns per its own weeks (red-zone stalls tagged twice, four Shrader kicks against Houston, five against Pittsburgh) and meets the defense that held New England 0-for-4 and Philadelphia to no touchdowns on three trips. The full story · the report's account, start to finish

The short version

Jacksonville won 42-7 as a 10-point home favorite, covering easily and sending a 45.5 total over with 49 combined points, and it did so on 17 pass attempts. The projection had the right winner and the right total and still graded a D, because it distributed Jacksonville's (correctly identified) efficiency over 31 attempts rather than letting 19 dropbacks produce 210 yards and three touchdowns. Indianapolis contributed three turnovers worth roughly -21 EPA, one of them a 92-yard pick-six, and never led for a single second.

How it unfolded

The Jaguars' first real possession started at their own 2 and went 103 yards in twelve plays; the drive-defining play was Lawrence's 46-yard strike to Brenton Strange at Q1 10:33 (+3.43 EPA), and it ended with a 12-yard touchdown to Brian Thomas on 3rd-and-12. That was the whole game in miniature: Jacksonville's first-down work was on the ground, its conversions came on one or two throws per series at a 9.8 air-yard depth.

Indianapolis's one incursion into scoring range died on an aborted snap at the JAX 49 (Q2 9:11, -5.81 EPA). Jacksonville needed four plays to score. Tuten's 39-yard run right tackle at Q2 2:00 made it 21-0. Then the sequence that buried it: Tuimoloau strip-sacked Lawrence at the JAX 40 with 1:03 left, Indianapolis recovered at the 38, moved to the 17 — and Jones' throw to Warren was intercepted by Rayuan Lane at the 8 and returned 92 yards (Q2 0:25, -11.02 EPA). 28-0 at the half. A failed 4th-and-3 sack at the Colts' 42 early in the third gave Jacksonville a 37-yard field; Rodriguez broke 28 up the middle. The rest was garbage time — 41 plays of it for Indianapolis.

Why the game diverged from the projection

Three inputs failed in the same direction. First, pass volume: the engine blended Jacksonville to a 0.549 pass rate; with a lead from the game's fifth minute they played at 0.407, and 0.381 on early downs. Second, the quality of those few throws: four completions of 20+ yards on 17 attempts, +10.2 CPOE, 0.371 EPA per dropback. Third, the Colts' front — blended at 4.16 yards per carry allowed, it surrendered 183 on 33 with two touchdown runs of 39 and 28 yards, 7.8 ypc to the right side.

On the other side, the model expected a chasing Colts offense to throw 33 times for 230. Jones threw 28 for 144 at a 4.6 adot with zero explosive completions, eight QB hits and four sacks across the offense. The blended 6.94 yards per attempt was always a stretch given Indianapolis entered at 5.83; the sack rate (blended 7.0%) was right, but the deficit then suppressed volume rather than inflating it.

The plan against the game

The pre-game plan nailed the shape and whiffed on the scale. It said Jacksonville would score early, run, shorten the game and cap Lawrence's attempts — all true, and the Washington under, the Lawrence under and the Tuten combined over came in together. What it did not foresee is that a run-lean 17-attempt game could produce 42 points; its ceiling for Jacksonville was 22-27, built on completion rate rather than explosives. The Colts half broke harder: the plan's "34-38 attempts, points arrive as field goals" became 30 attempts and zero field-goal tries, because the margin passed the point where chasing makes sense. Warren's role call (10 targets) and Treadwell's (snaps without yards) were right in mechanism regardless of the box-score result.

Player by player

Tuten is the lesson: 16 carries (projected 15.5) and 159 combined yards, with 61 YAC on two catches. When a favorite's script is known, the back's touches are the most forecastable thing on the board and the explosives are free upside. Lawrence beat a 249.5 yard line on 14 attempts at 15.0 a throw. Strange's 55 yards came on two targets; Washington, the nominal WR1, got two looks for 16.

For Indianapolis, Warren drew 10 targets and a 37.5% air-yards share and finished 4-18 with two interceptions thrown his way — the role is real, the output was catastrophic. Keenan Allen's 7-for-7, 67 yards on a 52.5% snap share is largely a garbage-time artifact. Taylor was stuffed on three of eight carries for 23 yards and lost the fumble that started the avalanche.

The defenses

Charting for pressure and coverage hasn't landed, so read the proxies. Jacksonville, tagged "no pass rush" three times this season and sackless against Houston in week 6, generated 4 sacks and 9 hits at a 13.6% blitz rate against a Colts line that false-started three times — that is protection failure as much as rush. Indianapolis actually won its own pass-rush snaps (3 sacks on 19 Lawrence dropbacks, 36.8% pressure including a strip-sack), which makes the 42 points more damning: the defense lost on explosives and on the ground, not for lack of getting home.

The blueprint

Indianapolis gets beaten two ways that recur: chunk runs (weeks 1, 2, 5, now this) and the occasional 40-yard throw (weeks 1, 2, 7), with its own offense handing the ball over in four of seven games. Jacksonville gets beaten by offenses that can throw explosively — DEN, NE and HOU all hit four or five 20-yard passes against them — and by its own flags (4.67 a game, 125 yards at Cincinnati). Indianapolis could not come close to trying that: a 4.5 adot and two deep attempts is the definition of a team that looks at a soft secondary and has no instrument to play it.

What the model should take from this

The engine's efficiency reads were fine; the volume allocation was not. When a favorite's pass rate drops 13 points below its season rate the moment it leads, the projection should shift yards into per-attempt and rushing, not shave the total. And the double-digit-dog pass bump needs a blowout cap: 56.7% is what a buried team actually throws.

The pick-six and the aborted snap are bounce and should not be carried forward at full weight. The giveaway rate that generated them, the 7.7% sack rate and the inability to complete anything past ten yards should be.
