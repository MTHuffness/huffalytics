The full story · the report's account, start to finish

The short version

Las Vegas won 38-33 on the road as a 1.5-point dog and the 46.5 total finished at 71. The Jets outgained them 456-342, won field EPA 0.385 to 0.162, ran for 166 and threw for 290 — and lost, because they went 2-for-6 inside the 20 while Las Vegas went 4-for-4 and added a 95-yard kickoff return touchdown. Every yard-level input the model had was roughly right; everything that converted yards into points went the other way.

How it unfolded

The first half was the plan's game. New York opened with a 31-yard shot to Wilson and kicked, Las Vegas answered with an 11-play, 75-yard drive capped by Bowers from 12, and the Jets kept grinding — 20- and 22-yard strikes to Adonai Mitchell, a 24-yard Allen run, a 4th-and-1 touchdown to Mitchell at Q2 6:23. Had Laube not returned the kickoff 95 yards at Q2 10:56 (5.96 EPA), New York leads by double digits at the break instead of 23-14; and had Vegas's wildcat 4th-and-1 at the NYJ 46 (Q2 3:58, -2.87 EPA) converted, the margin is smaller still.

The second half flipped on finishing and on the Jets' own drives. Vegas scored on three straight possessions — Bowers from two, Cousins' sneak after Brownlee's 11-yard DPI (free yards, never on the stat sheet), then the 84-yarder that began with Jeanty's 45-yard catch-and-run on the first snap of the fourth quarter. New York, in between, got one explosive drive (Allen 21 yards, Q3 8:26) and then three stalls: a holding penalty on Fashanu that erased a 28-yard Wilson completion, a Keenan sack, a three-and-out, and finally Mitchell's 47-yard catch that ended in a 31-yard field goal when the third-down throw to Taylor fell incomplete at the LV 13. Gay's 53-yarder at 0:31 made it a five-point game, which is why the last snap was a heave to the end zone and an interception rather than a field-goal drive.

Why the game diverged from the projection

The volume projections were close to exact — 121 combined plays against a 121.5 baseline, NYJ 27 carries against 27.1 projected, Geno 32 attempts against 30.6, Cousins 247 yards against 232.7. The divergence is entirely in points per opportunity. LV's 38 came from four red-zone trips that all ended in touchdowns (+0.474 EPA/play inside the 20), zero turnovers on 61 snaps, and seven points from the return unit. NYJ's 166 rush yards over a 116.6 projection is the one genuinely predictable miss: Allen hit five runs of 10+ against a front tagged for the run in weeks 4, 5 and 7, and with Hall inactive he owned 82.8% of the snaps.

The plan against the game

The plan found the right physical matchup and drew the wrong conclusion from it. New York did run at the Raiders, Allen did smash his number, and the Raiders' pocket was clean — but the plan's central claim, that Vegas gives up touchdowns at the goal line, was the one thing the Raiders defense did not do all afternoon. On the other side it had Vegas as a 38-45 attempt passing team and got 26 attempts and 33 carries from a club that trailed on 47 snaps. Two structural misses stand out as readable: the LV backfield had already inverted to Washington, and the Jets' target tree with Cooper Jr. out ran through Wilson and Mitchell, not a four-way split. The red-zone inversion and the final-play interception were bounce.

Player by player

Bowers is the Las Vegas passing game: 85.7% of snaps, 10 targets, 47.8% of air yards, both short touchdowns. Tucker, the projected vertical man, finished with one target and zero yards — a priority decision, not an injury. Jeanty's line is a role note, not a yardage read: two carries, four targets, one air yard, 59 YAC, and 45 of his 64 receiving yards on one play. Washington, a projected change-of-pace, took 25 carries. On the Jets' side, Mitchell with Cooper Jr. out put up 222 air yards and 142 receiving on a 15.0 team aDOT; Wilson led in targets with 12; Allen's 21-147-1 was the most straightforwardly predictable box score in the game.

The defenses

Neither pass rush showed up in the way its inputs suggested. New York blitzed on 46.7% of LV snaps — far above their 26.8% season rate — and got one sack and six hits; Cousins posted +0.607 EPA per dropback against it. Las Vegas hit Geno three times on 35 dropbacks (8.6% pressure) but got both of its sacks in critical spots: Crosby at the LV 21 before halftime, Keenan on the drive that followed the erased 28-yarder. Charting for this game has no coverage or personnel data — box counts and blitz rate are the only scheme numbers available, and the LV box of 6.39 faced is barely above league, which is to say they did not sell out to stop Allen and paid 6.1 ypc on the left edge for it.

The blueprint

Las Vegas gets beaten on the ground and over the top, and the evidence is three and four weeks deep. New York executed both halves of it and still lost because the Raiders' red zone — tagged soft in four prior weeks — held. That is the trap: a team can run the blueprint perfectly and lose on the facet the dossier explicitly flags as the least persistent. New York, conversely, gets beaten by itself: 0-for-3, 0-for-1 and now 2-for-6 inside the 20 across weeks 5, 6 and 8, a pass rush that generates nothing without help, and a penalty habit that erased a drive here. An opponent needs almost nothing special to beat them — yardage is cheap against this defense — only the ability to finish trips.

What the model should take from this

Three inputs deserve reweighting and one does not. Reweight: the Las Vegas backfield (Washington is the early-down back), the Jets' target tree without Cooper Jr. (Mitchell is a starter), and Las Vegas's willingness to run while trailing (46.8% pass rate in that state against a 62.9% league figure). Do not reweight either team's red-zone finishing off this game — a 4/4 and a 2/6 in one afternoon is noise by construction, and the engine is right to keep the touchdown split on the league constant. The broader lesson for sides and totals is older than this game: when both teams move the ball and one of them kicks, the loser's yardage props cash and the market's total does not describe the scoreboard.

Do not price a team's offense off an opponent's red-zone TD rate allowed. LV had conceded touchdowns on 78.3% of trips entering this game and allowed 2 of 6; NYJ's own 50% rate turned into 4 field goals. Red-zone rate is the least persistent input in the file — use it to fade extremes, not to forecast them.

Trigger: Any matchup where a team-total or anytime-TD angle rests mainly on an opponent's red-zone rank over six or seven games.

When a team wins field EPA by a wide margin and loses the red zone, the yardage props cash and the touchdown props and team total do not. NYJ: 456 yards, 290 passing, Mitchell 142, Allen 147 — and 33 points off six trips.
